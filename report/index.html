Scoring spoken grammar from 45 seconds of audio

A model that listens to an English speaker and grades their grammar from 0 to 5, built on Whisper transcripts, WavLM speech embeddings and a careful look at what the leaderboard actually measures.

Speech recognisers fix grammar by default, which erases the very errors we have to score. Solving that was the first step.

The task

Each clip is 45–60 s of spontaneous spoken English, rated on a 0–5 grammar scale (MOS Likert). There are 769 labelled training clips and 216 test clips. The leaderboard scores predictions with RMSE and Pearson correlation.

1Struggles with sentence structure; limited control of simple structures
2Simple structures with consistent basic errors; incomplete sentences
3Decent grasp of grammar or of structure, with errors in the other
4Strong control; occasional minor errors that don't impede understanding
5High accuracy, complex structures handled well, self-corrections

Approach

Each clip is described from two angles, then the two views are blended.

What was said

Whisper-large-v3-turbo transcribes the audio with a verbatim prompt full of fillers and mistakes, so it keeps the speaker's errors. From the transcript come length-robust text statistics plus ASR confidence (LightGBM) and DeBERTa-v3-large sentence embeddings (Ridge).

How it was said

Frozen speech encoders (WavLM-base-plus, WavLM-large and the Whisper encoder) are pooled over 20 s windows, taking the mean and standard deviation of middle layers, and fed to Ridge and SVR regressors. Audio is the strongest single view: fluency, rhythm and pronunciation track the grammar score closely.

Blend

A non-negative linear blend of every model's out-of-fold predictions, plus a score-0 gate: clips the WavLM model scores below 0.5 are set to exactly 0. It catches all 37 score-0 training clips with no false positives.

Validation that matches the test set

Speaker leakage

A speaker-verification model showed that training clips come in "speaker twins": the same voice with a similar score. With random folds about 80% of validation clips had a twin in training, so cross-validation rewarded models that recognise voices. Test clips are clearly farther from the training set (median nearest-speaker similarity 0.87, against 0.92 within train). The fix clusters speakers and keeps each cluster inside one fold, with the threshold tuned so validation clips sit exactly as far from training as test clips do.

Test-length crops

Every training clip is also cut, from the start, to four lengths drawn from the test clips' own durations. Models train on full clips plus crops and are validated on crops, with transcripts of the crops for the text models. This was the single biggest leaderboard gain, from 0.3454 to 0.3350.

Results on the training data

The out-of-fold figures are the honest ones: every clip is predicted by models that never saw its speaker, at test-like lengths. The in-sample figures refit every model on all training data, and the RBF-kernel SVRs nearly memorise what they were trained on, hence 0.112.

What the leaderboard measures

Adding a constant c to every prediction leaves Pearson unchanged and moves RMSE in a known way, so two shifted copies of v7 test what the score is made of.

SubmissionPublic score
v70.3350
v7 + 0.20.3849
v7 − 0.20.3305

Not pure RMSE. For pure RMSE the two shifted scores would satisfy:

LB(+)² + LB(−)² = 2·LB(v7)² + 2·0.2² = 0.3045
observed: 0.3849² + 0.3305² = 0.2574

Part of the score doesn't move under a shift: the Pearson component. Better models improve both parts together, which is why the score kept falling as the blend improved.

A test-set bias. Solving the same equations under every plausible mix of RMSE and Pearson gives the same answer: v7's test predictions are 0.118 too high on average. The predicted score after removing it was 0.3259–0.3264, and the actual result was 0.3262.

Where it comes from. It is the same for short (+0.10) and full-length (+0.13) test clips, and cross-validation shows no bias (−0.008). The test speakers simply score about 0.25 lower on average than the training speakers (implied mean 3.23 against 3.48), so a constant shift is the right correction. The final submission is v7 minus 0.118, clipped to [0, 5].

Limitations

Only 769 training clips, and the public leaderboard scores 216 test clips, so differences under about 0.01 are mostly noise. Predictions regress toward the mean, so scores of 2 and 5 carry the largest errors. The bias correction assumes the private test speakers resemble the public ones.
