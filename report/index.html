Scoring spoken grammar from 45 seconds of audio

Aryan Awasthi, SHL Hiring Assessment 2026, October 2026

he don'tdoesn't like it

The speaker said "don't". A standard speech recogniser writes "doesn't", and the error this system has to score disappears from the transcript. Keeping it was the first problem to solve.

Abstract

We predict a 0–5 grammar score for 45–60 s clips of spontaneous spoken English. The system combines Whisper transcripts decoded with a verbatim prompt, frozen WavLM and Whisper-encoder speech embeddings, hidden states of an audio language model (Voxtral-Mini-3B), and a gated non-negative blend of three linear and kernel regressors. Two validation corrections mattered more than any model choice: grouping cross-validation folds by speaker, and training on crops that match the shorter test clips. On the training data the final blend reaches an out-of-fold RMSE of 0.516 (Pearson 0.909). On the public leaderboard it scores 0.3207, after removing a test-set bias and restoring the compressed spread of the full-length test predictions, both measured with probe submissions.

1Task and data

Each clip is rated on a 0–5 grammar scale (MOS Likert). There are 769 labelled training clips and 216 test clips. The competition scores predictions with RMSE and Pearson correlation.

1Struggles with sentence structure; limited control of simple structures
2Simple structures with consistent basic errors; incomplete sentences
3Decent grasp of grammar or of structure, with errors in the other
4Strong control; occasional minor errors that don't impede understanding
5High accuracy; complex structures handled well; self-corrections

Test clips are shorter and often end mid-sentence. In training, short clips do score lower, so a model can learn that less audio means a lower score. Cutting full-length clips to 48 s lowered a WavLM model's predictions by 0.14, with the same speakers and the same grammar.

2Method

Each clip is described from two angles, and the two views are combined at the end.

2.1 Transcript features

Whisper-large-v3-turbo transcribes each clip with a verbatim prompt full of fillers and mistakes, which keeps the speaker's errors in the transcript. Two models use the text: length-robust statistics plus ASR confidence (LightGBM), and DeBERTa-v3-large sentence embeddings (Ridge).

2.2 Acoustic features

Frozen speech encoders (WavLM-base-plus, WavLM-large and the Whisper encoder) are applied over 20 s windows, pooling the mean and standard deviation of their middle layers. Ridge and RBF-kernel SVR regressors are trained on the pooled embeddings. Audio is the strongest single view, because fluency, rhythm and pronunciation track the grammar score closely.

Voxtral-Mini-3B is an audio language model: a Whisper-style encoder feeding a 3B-parameter language model. It hears the clip, followed by the scoring rubric, and the hidden states of its language model over the audio tokens (layers at 20-60% of depth, mean and standard deviation over the tokens that cover real audio) are pooled like the other embeddings. Because it listens rather than reads a transcript, it can register errors that speech recognition smooths over. Its features are wide (6144 values per clip), and a Ridge over them together with the WavLM, WavLM-large and Whisper-encoder embeddings is the best single model (CV RMSE 0.538, against 0.579 for an SVR on Voxtral and WavLM-large). The idea of using an audio language model's hidden states as features comes from another participant's public solution; the implementation and its training on test-length crops are ours.

2.3 Blend and score-0 gate

A non-negative linear blend with an intercept is fitted to the out-of-fold predictions of three models, one per kind of signal (Table 4). Three models beat the earlier four (CV RMSE 0.5158 against 0.5207) and eight (0.5280). Clips that the WavLM model scores below 0.5 are set to exactly 0, which catches all 37 score-0 training clips with no false positives.

3Validation design

3.1 Speaker leakage

A speaker-verification model shows that training clips come in pairs: the same voice, with a similar score. With random folds, about 80% of validation clips had such a twin in the training folds, so cross-validation rewarded models that recognise voices. Test clips sit clearly farther from the training set (median nearest-speaker similarity 0.87, against 0.92 within train). We cluster speakers and keep each cluster inside one fold. The clustering threshold is tuned so that validation clips are as far from their training folds as test clips are from train.

3.2 Test-length crops

Every training clip is also cut, from the start, to four lengths drawn from the test clips' own durations. Models train on full clips plus crops and are validated on crops, and the text models use transcripts of the crops. This was the largest single leaderboard improvement, from 0.3454 to 0.3350.

5Leaderboard analysis

5.1 What the metric measures

Adding a constant to every prediction leaves Pearson correlation unchanged and moves RMSE in a known way. Two shifted copies of v7 therefore separate the two parts of the score.

For pure RMSE the first three rows would satisfy

LB(+0.2)² + LB(−0.2)² = 2·LB(v7)² + 2·0.2² = 0.3045
observed: 0.3849² + 0.3305² = 0.2574

Part of the score therefore does not move under a shift; that part is the Pearson component. Solving the same equations under every plausible mix of RMSE and Pearson gives the same answer: v7's test predictions are 0.118 too high on average. The predicted score after removing the bias was 0.3259–0.3264, and the observed score was 0.3262.

5.2 Origin of the bias

Cross-validation on the training clips shows no bias (−0.008), and further probes show the bias is the same for short and full-length test clips. Shifting either group by a further 0.1 made the score worse (Table 5). The most consistent explanation is that the test speakers score about 0.25 lower on average than the training speakers (implied mean 3.23, against 3.48 in train). A constant shift is the appropriate correction for it.

5.3 The spread of the test predictions

v7's test predictions are also compressed: their standard deviation is 0.80, against 1.12 for its out-of-fold predictions on the training clips. Stretching predictions around their mean, mean + s·(pred − mean), leaves Pearson unchanged, so three probes test where the compression is an error (Table 6). The gain is all in the full-length clips. The short clips' narrower spread (0.65, against 1.04 for full-length clips) is real, and stretching them costs score. The final submission applies both corrections to the four-model blend: the same test mean as v7 minus 0.118 (a shift of −0.1254, since the blend's test mean is 0.0074 above v7's), then the full-length clips (over 55 s) stretched by 1.15 around the mean of all predictions, clipped to [0, 5].

7Limitations and conclusion

The training set is small (769 clips) and the public leaderboard scores only 216 clips, so differences below about 0.01 are mostly noise. Predictions regress toward the mean, which leaves the largest errors at scores 2 and 5. The bias and spread corrections assume that the private test speakers resemble the public ones.

Within those limits, the result shows that a reliable grammar score for spoken English comes less from a larger model than from measuring the right thing. That meant keeping the speaker's errors in the transcript, validating on unseen speakers at test-like clip lengths, and reading the leaderboard metric closely enough to correct a bias that cross-validation could not see.
